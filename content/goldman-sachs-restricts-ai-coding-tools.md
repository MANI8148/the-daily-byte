## Goldman Sachs pulls the plug on AI coding for non-engineers

Goldman Sachs is rolling back widespread access to AI coding assistants, restricting tools like Claude Code and GitHub Copilot to engineering teams only. The move, confirmed by internal memos this week, signals a significant shift in how large financial institutions are approaching generative AI adoption — moving from experimentation to controlled deployment.

The decision affects traders and bankers who previously had access to these tools for automating routine tasks, writing scripts, and prototyping workflows. Access now remains reserved for software engineers within the firm's technology divisions. As recently as July, Goldman Sachs CIO Marco Argenti outlined a vision of AI-Augmented development across the firm, making this pivot particularly notable.

## Why limit access? Governance, risk, and compliance take center stage

Financial institutions operate under some of the strictest regulatory scrutiny in the corporate world. Goldman Sachs has not published detailed reasoning, but the restriction aligns with a broader industry trend of tightening AI governance. Trading desks and client-facing operations demand audit trails, model transparency, and data privacy guarantees — areas where unrestricted access to third-party coding assistants creates compliance headaches.

A spokesperson for the firm emphasized that the change ensures "AI-assisted development occurs within approved environments with appropriate oversight." For a bank handling trillions in assets and sensitive client data, the cost of an unvetted code generation slip-up outweighs the productivity gains of widespread tool access.

## The tools at the center of the change

Two names dominate the conversation: Claude Code, Anthropic's command-line coding agent, and GitHub Copilot, the longtime AI pair-programming staple. Both allow developers to generate boilerplate code, refactor functions, and answer questions about unfamiliar libraries using natural language prompts.

Goldman Sachs employees reported receiving notifications this week that their Copilot and Claude Code licenses would be deactivated unless they belonged to engineering groups. The firm is not discontinuing the partnerships outright but is reassigning seats to ensure only authorized users maintain access.

## What this means for traders and bankers

For the firm's sales and trading divisions, the loss of direct AI coding access means returning to traditional development channels. Requests for custom scripts or automation now route through engineering queues, potentially slowing prototyping cycles that had accelerated under previous policies.

Some traders had come to rely on Copilot for quick Python snippets — calculating moving averages, formatting Bloomberg API responses, or restructuring CSV data for risk models. Without direct access, the turnaround time for these tasks lengthens, though the firm maintains that dedicated engineering support remains available.

## What this means for Goldman's engineering teams

Engineering groups within Goldman Sachs retain full access, and the firm says it is investing in internal AI tooling to fill the gap. CIO Marco Argenti, who has been vocal about the firm's AI strategy, reportedly directed teams to build proprietary assistants that comply with Goldman's security and data retention policies.

This approach mirrors a growing pattern: rather than relying solely on off-the-shelf AI products, large enterprises are developing in-house capabilities tailored to their specific risk profiles. For engineers, the shift means more responsibility for vetting, maintaining, and securing the tools their colleagues use.

## A wider trend: from AI experimentation to operational control

Goldman Sachs is not alone in reining in AI access. Earlier this year, JPMorgan Chase limited employee use of certain generative AI features following internal reviews. Tech giants including Apple and Samsung have imposed similar restrictions after employees leaked sensitive data to public-facing large language models.

The common thread is a maturation of the market. The initial rush to put AI tools in every employee's hands has given way to more deliberate strategies focused on risk management, data governance, and measurable ROI. Goldman's move represents one of the highest-profile examples yet of this transition.

## The road ahead for enterprise AI adoption

Goldman Sachs will likely continue expanding AI capabilities, but through controlled channels. The firm's internal AI platform, already in use for certain analytics and workflow automation tasks, may see broader rollout as a compliant alternative to third-party coding assistants.

For now, the message is clear: AI remains a priority, but its deployment in sensitive environments will follow stricter guardrails. Traders and bankers who hoped AI would streamline their daily workflows will need to adjust, while engineering teams gain both privilege and responsibility in equal measure.
